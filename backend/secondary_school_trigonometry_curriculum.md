**Purpose:** Provide structured, learner-friendly teaching content for an AI-powered personalized Trigonometry learning platform.

**Target learners:** Secondary-school learners studying introductory geometry and trigonometry.

**Teaching approach:** Explain concepts in simple language, demonstrate worked examples step by step, use diagrams or descriptions of diagrams, check understanding regularly, and progress from concrete examples to independent practice.

**Progression rule for the learning platform:** Learners should study lessons in the configured order. # WEEK 1 — FOUNDATION

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Meaning of trigonometry, angles, types of triangles, and properties of triangles.

**Prior knowledge:** Basic shapes, counting, measurement, addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and simple fractions.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Explain the meaning and purpose of trigonometry in simple terms.
2. Identify and measure angles in degrees.
3. Classify angles as acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex, or complete angles.
4. Identify triangles and classify them by their sides and angles.
5. State and use the angle-sum property of a triangle.
6. Recognize that the longest side of a triangle is opposite its largest angle.
7. Solve simple problems involving missing angles in triangles.

## Lesson 1.1 — What is trigonometry?

### Meaning

Trigonometry is a branch of mathematics that studies relationships between the angles and sides of triangles. The word is often associated with measuring triangles.

Trigonometry helps us calculate a side or an angle when we know enough information about a triangle. It is useful when direct measurement is difficult.

### Uses of trigonometry

Trigonometry can help people:
- Estimate the height of a tree, building, or hill.
- Calculate distances that are difficult to measure directly.
- Support surveying, construction, architecture, navigation, engineering, astronomy, and mapping.
- Analyse slopes, ramps, roofs, and lines of sight.

### Example

Suppose a learner wants to estimate the height of a tall tree. Measuring the tree from its base to its top may be difficult. If the learner can measure a horizontal distance from the tree and the angle from the ground to the top, trigonometry can help estimate the height.

At this stage, learners only need to understand the idea; the calculation methods will be taught in later weeks.

### Check your understanding
1. In your own words, what does trigonometry study?
2. Give two situations in which trigonometry may be useful.
3. Why might trigonometry be more practical than trying to climb a tall tree to measure its height?

## Lesson 1.2 — Angles and their measurement

### Meaning of an angle

An angle is formed when two rays or line segments meet at a common endpoint. The common endpoint is called the **vertex**. The two rays are the **arms** of the angle.

Angles are commonly measured in degrees, written using the symbol `°`. One complete turn is `360°`, a half-turn is `180°`, and a quarter-turn is `90°`.

### Types of angles

- **Zero angle:** `0°`
- **Acute angle:** greater than `0°` but less than `90°`
- **Right angle:** exactly `90°`
- **Obtuse angle:** greater than `90°` but less than `180°`
- **Straight angle:** exactly `180°`
- **Reflex angle:** greater than `180°` but less than `360°`
- **Complete angle:** exactly `360°`

### Worked examples

**Example 1:** Classify `35°`.

Since `0° < 35° < 90°`, it is an acute angle.

**Example 2:** Classify `125°`.

Since `90° < 125° < 180°`, it is an obtuse angle.

**Example 3:** A learner turns through a half-turn. How many degrees is the turn?

A half-turn is `180°`.

### Measuring angles with a protractor

1. Place the centre mark of the protractor on the vertex.
2. Align the baseline with one arm of the angle.
3. Read the scale beginning at `0°` on the aligned arm.
4. Record the measurement and include the degree symbol.

### Check your understanding
Classify each angle: `25°`, `90°`, `145°`, `180°`, `230°`, and `360°`.

## Lesson 1.3 — Types of triangles by their sides

A triangle is a closed two-dimensional shape with three straight sides, three vertices, and three interior angles.

Triangles can be classified by their side lengths.

### Equilateral triangle

An equilateral triangle has three equal sides. Its three interior angles are equal, and each is `60°`.

### Isosceles triangle

An isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides. The angles opposite the equal sides are equal.

### Scalene triangle

A scalene triangle has three sides of different lengths. Its angles are also different.

### Worked example

A triangle has side lengths `5 cm`, `5 cm`, and `8 cm`. Two sides are equal, so the triangle is isosceles.

### Check your understanding
1. What is the defining property of an equilateral triangle?
2. A triangle has sides of `4 cm`, `6 cm`, and `7 cm`. Classify it by its sides.
3. What can you say about the base angles of an isosceles triangle?

## Lesson 1.4 — Types of triangles by their angles

Triangles can also be classified by their interior angles.

- **Acute-angled triangle:** all three angles are less than `90°`.
- **Right-angled triangle:** one angle is exactly `90°`.
- **Obtuse-angled triangle:** one angle is greater than `90°`.

A triangle cannot have two right angles, and it cannot have more than one obtuse angle, because the sum of its interior angles is `180°`.

### Worked examples

**Example 1:** A triangle has angles `50°`, `60°`, and `70°`. All are less than `90°`, so it is acute-angled.

**Example 2:** A triangle has angles `90°`, `35°`, and `55°`. It is right-angled.

**Example 3:** A triangle has angles `110°`, `30°`, and `40°`. It is obtuse-angled.

## Lesson 1.5 — Properties of triangles

### Property 1: Interior angles add up to `180°`

For any triangle with interior angles `A`, `B`, and `C`:

`A + B + C = 180°`

### Worked example

A triangle has two angles measuring `48°` and `67°`. Find the third angle.

Step 1: Let the missing angle be `x`.

Step 2: Use the angle-sum property:

`48° + 67° + x = 180°`

Step 3: Add the known angles:

`115° + x = 180°`

Step 4: Subtract `115°` from both sides:

`x = 65°`

The missing angle is `65°`.

### Property 2: Exterior angle property

An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles that are not adjacent to it.

For example, if the two opposite interior angles are `45°` and `70°`, the exterior angle is:

`45° + 70° = 115°`

### Property 3: Side-angle relationship

In a triangle, the longest side is opposite the largest angle, and the shortest side is opposite the smallest angle.

### Week 1 summary
- Trigonometry studies relationships between sides and angles of triangles.
- Angles are measured in degrees in this introductory course.
- Triangles can be classified by their sides or by their angles.
- The interior angles of a triangle add up to `180°`.
- The exterior angle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles.

### Week 1 practice
1. Classify an angle of `72°`.
2. Classify a triangle with sides `6 cm`, `6 cm`, and `6 cm`.
3. Find the third angle of a triangle whose other angles are `42°` and `83°`.
4. A triangle has angles `90°`, `25°`, and `65°`. Classify it by its angles.
5. The two opposite interior angles of a triangle's exterior angle are `38°` and `64°`. Find the exterior angle.
6. Explain one real-life use of trigonometry.

### Week 1 mastery check — answer key for teacher/platform

1. Acute.
2. Equilateral.
3. `180° − 42° − 83° = 55°`.
4. Right-angled.
5. `38° + 64° = 102°`.
6. Accept a correct example, such as estimating a building's height or supporting surveying.

# WEEK 2 — RIGHT-ANGLED TRIANGLES

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Right angle, hypotenuse, opposite side, and adjacent side.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Identify a right angle in a triangle.
2. Identify the hypotenuse correctly.
3. Identify the opposite and adjacent sides relative to a chosen acute angle.
4. Explain why opposite and adjacent labels depend on the reference angle.
5. Label a right-angled triangle accurately.

## Lesson 2.1 — Understanding a right-angled triangle

A right-angled triangle is a triangle containing one angle of exactly `90°`. A small square is often drawn at the right-angle vertex to show this.

The two sides that meet to form the right angle are the shorter perpendicular sides. The side opposite the right angle is the **hypotenuse**.

### Important rule

The hypotenuse is always:
- Opposite the `90°` angle.
- The longest side of the right-angled triangle.
- The side that does not form one of the two arms of the right angle.

Do not identify the hypotenuse by guessing which side looks longest in a drawing. Find the right angle first, then identify the side opposite it.

## Lesson 2.2 — Opposite and adjacent sides

Choose one of the two acute angles in a right-angled triangle as the **reference angle**. The labels opposite and adjacent are defined relative to that angle.

- **Opposite side:** the side directly across from the reference angle.
- **Adjacent side:** the side next to the reference angle that is not the hypotenuse.
- **Hypotenuse:** the side opposite the right angle.

### Worked labelling example

Imagine triangle `ABC`, where angle `C = 90°`. Therefore, side `AB` is the hypotenuse.

If the reference angle is `A`:
- Opposite side: `BC`
- Adjacent side: `AC`
- Hypotenuse: `AB`

If the reference angle changes to `B`:
- Opposite side: `AC`
- Adjacent side: `BC`
- Hypotenuse: `AB`

Notice that the hypotenuse remains the same, but the opposite and adjacent sides switch.

### Common mistake

Calling a particular side “the opposite side” without stating the reference angle can lead to confusion. Always mark or name the reference angle first.

## Lesson 2.3 — Reading and labelling triangle diagrams

When a problem gives a right-angled triangle:

1. Find the right-angle marker.
2. Identify the hypotenuse as the side opposite the right angle.
3. Find the acute angle named in the question.
4. Label the side across from that angle as opposite.
5. Label the remaining non-hypotenuse side as adjacent.
6. Check that each side has been labelled consistently.

### Guided examples

**Example 1:** Triangle `PQR` has `angle Q = 90°`. Which side is the hypotenuse?

The side opposite angle `Q` is `PR`; therefore, `PR` is the hypotenuse.

**Example 2:** In the same triangle, the reference angle is `P`. Identify opposite and adjacent sides.

- Opposite: `QR`
- Adjacent: `PQ`
- Hypotenuse: `PR`

**Example 3:** The reference angle changes to `R`. Identify opposite and adjacent sides.

- Opposite: `PQ`
- Adjacent: `QR`
- Hypotenuse: `PR`

### Week 2 summary
- A right-angled triangle has one `90°` angle.
- The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle.
- Opposite and adjacent depend on the selected reference angle.
- Correct labelling is essential before using trigonometric ratios.

### Week 2 practice
1. What is the hypotenuse?
2. In triangle `XYZ`, `angle Y = 90°`. Name the hypotenuse.
3. In triangle `XYZ`, with `angle Y = 90°`, identify opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse relative to angle `X`.
4. If the reference angle changes from `X` to `Z`, which two non-hypotenuse labels switch?
5. Explain the correct steps for labelling the sides of a right-angled triangle.

### Week 2 mastery check — answer key

1. The side opposite the right angle.
2. `XZ`.
3. Opposite `YZ`; adjacent `XY`; hypotenuse `XZ`.
4. Opposite and adjacent switch; the hypotenuse stays the same.
5. Find the right angle, identify the hypotenuse, select the reference angle, then label opposite and adjacent.

# WEEK 3 — PYTHAGORAS' THEOREM

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Finding unknown sides and applications.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. State Pythagoras' theorem.
2. Recognize that it applies to right-angled triangles.
3. Find the hypotenuse when both shorter sides are known.
4. Find a missing shorter side when the hypotenuse and other shorter side are known.
5. Include correct units in answers and check whether the answer is reasonable.

## Lesson 3.1 — The theorem

Pythagoras' theorem states that, in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

If the shorter sides are `a` and `b`, and the hypotenuse is `c`:

`a² + b² = c²`

This relationship applies only to right-angled triangles.

### What does “squared” mean?

Squaring a number means multiplying it by itself:
- `3² = 3 × 3 = 9`
- `5² = 5 × 5 = 25`
- `10² = 10 × 10 = 100`

The square root reverses squaring:
- `√25 = 5`
- `√100 = 10`

## Lesson 3.2 — Finding the hypotenuse

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has shorter sides of `3 cm` and `4 cm`. Find the hypotenuse.

Step 1: Write the formula.

`a² + b² = c²`

Step 2: Substitute the values.

`3² + 4² = c²`

Step 3: Square each value.

`9 + 16 = c²`

Step 4: Add.

`25 = c²`

Step 5: Take the positive square root because a length is positive.

`c = √25 = 5 cm`

The hypotenuse is `5 cm`.

## Lesson 3.3 — Finding a shorter side

When the hypotenuse and one shorter side are known, rearrange the theorem.

If the unknown side is `a`:

`a² = c² − b²`

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse `13 cm` and one shorter side `5 cm`. Find the other shorter side.

Step 1:

`a² + 5² = 13²`

Step 2:

`a² + 25 = 169`

Step 3: Subtract `25`.

`a² = 144`

Step 4: Take the positive square root.

`a = √144 = 12 cm`

The missing side is `12 cm`.

### Check the result

The sides `5`, `12`, and `13` satisfy the theorem because:

`5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13²`

## Lesson 3.4 — Real-life applications

### Example: A ladder against a wall

A ladder reaches a point `4 m` above the ground. Its foot is `3 m` from the wall. Assuming the wall and ground are perpendicular, find the ladder's length.

The wall and ground form the shorter sides of a right-angled triangle. The ladder is the hypotenuse.

`L² = 3² + 4²`

`L² = 9 + 16 = 25`

`L = √25 = 5 m`

The ladder is `5 m` long.

### Example: Checking a triangle

A triangle has side lengths `6 cm`, `8 cm`, and `10 cm`. Is it right-angled?

Use the longest side as the possible hypotenuse:

`6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100`

`10² = 100`

The values are equal, so the triangle is right-angled.

### Common mistakes
- Applying the theorem to a triangle that is not right-angled.
- Treating the shortest side as the hypotenuse.
- Adding the squares when the unknown is a shorter side.
- Forgetting the square root at the final step.
- Giving an answer without units.

### Week 3 practice
1. Find the hypotenuse when the shorter sides are `6 cm` and `8 cm`.
2. Find the missing shorter side when the hypotenuse is `10 cm` and the other shorter side is `6 cm`.
3. A right-angled triangle has shorter sides `5 m` and `12 m`. Find the hypotenuse.
4. A ladder is `10 m` long and its foot is `6 m` from a vertical wall. How high does it reach?
5. Determine whether side lengths `9 cm`, `12 cm`, and `15 cm` form a right-angled triangle.
6. Explain why Pythagoras' theorem cannot be applied directly to every triangle.

### Week 3 mastery check — answer key

1. `√(6² + 8²) = √100 = 10 cm`.
2. `√(10² − 6²) = √64 = 8 cm`.
3. `√(5² + 12²) = √169 = 13 m`.
4. Height `= √(10² − 6²) = 8 m`.
5. Yes, because `9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15²`.
6. The theorem is specifically for right-angled triangles.

# WEEK 4 — TRIGONOMETRIC RATIOS

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Sine, cosine, tangent, and SOH-CAH-TOA.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Define sine, cosine, and tangent for an acute angle in a right-angled triangle.
2. Recall SOH-CAH-TOA.
3. Select the appropriate ratio from the sides given and required.
4. Use a calculator in degree mode to evaluate basic trigonometric ratios.
5. Recognize that the ratios depend on the chosen angle.

## Lesson 4.1 — Why use trigonometric ratios?

Pythagoras' theorem connects the three side lengths of a right-angled triangle. Trigonometric ratios connect a chosen acute angle with ratios of side lengths.

Before selecting a ratio, label the opposite side, adjacent side, and hypotenuse relative to the given angle.

## Lesson 4.2 — Sine

Sine is abbreviated as `sin`.

For an acute angle `θ` in a right-angled triangle:

`sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse`

The memory aid **SOH** means:

**S**ine = **O**pposite / **H**ypotenuse.

### Example

Relative to angle `θ`, the opposite side is `3 cm` and the hypotenuse is `5 cm`.

`sin θ = 3/5 = 0.6`

## Lesson 4.3 — Cosine

Cosine is abbreviated as `cos`.

`cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse`

The memory aid **CAH** means:

**C**osine = **A**djacent / **H**ypotenuse.

### Example

Relative to angle `θ`, the adjacent side is `4 cm` and the hypotenuse is `5 cm`.

`cos θ = 4/5 = 0.8`

## Lesson 4.4 — Tangent

Tangent is abbreviated as `tan`.

`tan θ = opposite / adjacent`

The memory aid **TOA** means:

**T**angent = **O**pposite / **A**djacent.

### Example

Relative to angle `θ`, the opposite side is `3 cm` and the adjacent side is `4 cm`.

`tan θ = 3/4 = 0.75`

## Lesson 4.5 — Using SOH-CAH-TOA to choose a ratio

Remember:

- `sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse`
- `cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse`
- `tan θ = opposite / adjacent`

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has, relative to angle `θ`, an opposite side of `6 cm`, an adjacent side of `8 cm`, and a hypotenuse of `10 cm`. Find the three ratios.

`sin θ = 6/10 = 0.6`

`cos θ = 8/10 = 0.8`

`tan θ = 6/8 = 0.75`

### Choosing a ratio

1. Mark the angle in question.
2. Label opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse.
3. Identify which two sides are known or involved.
4. Choose the ratio containing those sides.
5. Substitute carefully.

## Lesson 4.6 — Calculator skills and degrees

For the introductory problems in this course, angles are usually given in degrees. Set the scientific calculator to **DEG** mode unless a question specifically asks for radians.

For example, in degree mode:
- `sin(30°) = 0.5`
- `cos(60°) = 0.5`
- `tan(45°) = 1`

Calculator button sequences vary by model. Learners should check the display for `DEG` before evaluating an angle in degrees.

### Common mistakes
- Swapping the opposite and adjacent sides.
- Using the hypotenuse in the tangent ratio.
- Forgetting that the side labels depend on the selected angle.
- Using radians mode when the question uses degrees.
- Confusing `sin θ` with `sin⁻¹ θ`; inverse ratios are taught in Week 6.

### Week 4 practice
1. Write the formula for sine.
2. Write the formula for cosine.
3. Write the formula for tangent.
4. If opposite `= 5` and hypotenuse `= 13`, find `sin θ`.
5. If adjacent `= 12` and hypotenuse `= 13`, find `cos θ`.
6. If opposite `= 9` and adjacent `= 12`, find `tan θ`.
7. Which ratio uses opposite and hypotenuse?
8. Why must the reference angle be marked before choosing a ratio?

### Week 4 mastery check — answer key

1. `sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse`.
2. `cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse`.
3. `tan θ = opposite/adjacent`.
4. `5/13 ≈ 0.3846`.
5. `12/13 ≈ 0.9231`.
6. `9/12 = 0.75`.
7. Sine.
8. The opposite and adjacent labels depend on the reference angle.

# WEEK 5 — FINDING UNKNOWN SIDES USING TRIGONOMETRIC RATIOS

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Using sine, cosine, and tangent to calculate unknown sides.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Identify the known angle and sides in a right-angled triangle.
2. Select the correct trigonometric ratio.
3. Rearrange the ratio to make the unknown side the subject.
4. Calculate an unknown side using a scientific calculator.
5. Round answers as instructed and include units.

## Lesson 5.1 — Finding an opposite side

If the angle and hypotenuse are known, use sine:

`sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse`

Rearrange:

`opposite = hypotenuse × sin θ`

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has an angle of `30°` and a hypotenuse of `12 cm`. Find the side opposite the angle.

Step 1: Identify the ratio. Opposite and hypotenuse are involved, so use sine.

`sin 30° = opposite / 12`

Step 2: Multiply both sides by `12`.

`opposite = 12 × sin 30°`

Step 3: Evaluate in degree mode.

`opposite = 12 × 0.5 = 6 cm`

## Lesson 5.2 — Finding an adjacent side

If the angle and hypotenuse are known, use cosine:

`cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse`

Rearrange:

`adjacent = hypotenuse × cos θ`

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has an angle of `60°` and a hypotenuse of `14 cm`. Find the adjacent side.

`adjacent = 14 × cos 60°`

`adjacent = 14 × 0.5 = 7 cm`

## Lesson 5.3 — Finding an opposite or adjacent side using tangent

Tangent relates the opposite and adjacent sides:

`tan θ = opposite / adjacent`

If the adjacent side is known:

`opposite = adjacent × tan θ`

If the opposite side is known:

`adjacent = opposite / tan θ`

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has an angle of `45°` and an adjacent side of `8 m`. Find the opposite side.

`opposite = 8 × tan 45°`

Since `tan 45° = 1`:

`opposite = 8 × 1 = 8 m`

## Lesson 5.4 — Choosing the right method

### Worked example

A right-angled triangle has an angle of `40°` and a hypotenuse of `15 cm`. Find the side opposite the angle, correct to one decimal place.

Opposite and hypotenuse are involved, so use sine:

`sin 40° = opposite / 15`

`opposite = 15 × sin 40°`

Using a calculator in degree mode:

`opposite ≈ 15 × 0.6428 = 9.6417... cm`

To one decimal place, the answer is `9.6 cm`.

### Practical procedure
1. Draw or inspect the right-angled triangle.
2. Mark the given angle.
3. Label all known sides relative to that angle.
4. Identify the required side.
5. Choose sine, cosine, or tangent.
6. Rearrange before substituting if needed.
7. Calculate in the correct angle mode.
8. Round only at the end, unless the question says otherwise.
9. Include the unit and check whether the result is reasonable.

### Week 5 practice
1. An angle is `30°` and the hypotenuse is `10 cm`. Find the opposite side.
2. An angle is `60°` and the hypotenuse is `20 m`. Find the adjacent side.
3. An angle is `45°` and the adjacent side is `9 cm`. Find the opposite side.
4. An angle is `35°` and the hypotenuse is `12 cm`. Find the opposite side to one decimal place.
5. An angle is `25°` and the hypotenuse is `16 cm`. Find the adjacent side to one decimal place.
6. Explain how you choose between sine, cosine, and tangent.

### Week 5 mastery check — answer key

1. `10 × sin 30° = 5 cm`.
2. `20 × cos 60° = 10 m`.
3. `9 × tan 45° = 9 cm`.
4. `12 × sin 35° ≈ 6.9 cm`.
5. `16 × cos 25° ≈ 14.5 cm`.
6. Mark the reference angle and select the ratio containing the known side and the required side.

# WEEK 6 — FINDING UNKNOWN ANGLES USING INVERSE TRIGONOMETRY

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Inverse sine, inverse cosine, and inverse tangent.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Explain when an inverse trigonometric function is needed.
2. Distinguish `sin`, `cos`, and `tan` from `sin⁻¹`, `cos⁻¹`, and `tan⁻¹`.
3. Calculate an unknown acute angle using two known sides.
4. Use a calculator in degree mode.
5. Round angle answers as instructed.

## Lesson 6.1 — What is an inverse trigonometric function?

In earlier lessons, learners used an angle to calculate a side. Sometimes two side lengths are known and the angle is unknown. An inverse trigonometric function can be used to find that angle.

The notation:
- `sin⁻¹(x)` or `arcsin(x)` means inverse sine.
- `cos⁻¹(x)` or `arccos(x)` means inverse cosine.
- `tan⁻¹(x)` or `arctan(x)` means inverse tangent.

**Important:** In this context, `sin⁻¹(x)` does not mean `1/sin(x)`. It means the inverse operation used to find an angle from a sine ratio.

## Lesson 6.2 — Finding an angle using inverse sine

When the opposite side and hypotenuse are known:

`sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse`

Therefore:

`θ = sin⁻¹(opposite / hypotenuse)`

### Worked example

The opposite side is `5 cm` and the hypotenuse is `10 cm`. Find angle `θ`.

Step 1:

`sin θ = 5/10 = 0.5`

Step 2:

`θ = sin⁻¹(0.5)`

Step 3: In degree mode:

`θ = 30°`

## Lesson 6.3 — Finding an angle using inverse cosine

When the adjacent side and hypotenuse are known:

`cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse`

Therefore:

`θ = cos⁻¹(adjacent / hypotenuse)`

### Worked example

The adjacent side is `8 cm` and the hypotenuse is `10 cm`.

`cos θ = 8/10 = 0.8`

`θ = cos⁻¹(0.8)`

`θ ≈ 36.9°`

## Lesson 6.4 — Finding an angle using inverse tangent

When the opposite and adjacent sides are known:

`tan θ = opposite / adjacent`

Therefore:

`θ = tan⁻¹(opposite / adjacent)`

### Worked example

The opposite side is `3 cm` and the adjacent side is `4 cm`.

`tan θ = 3/4 = 0.75`

`θ = tan⁻¹(0.75)`

`θ ≈ 36.9°`

## Lesson 6.5 — Calculator use and checking

For inverse trigonometry in this course:
1. Ensure the calculator is in degree mode.
2. Find the ratio of the relevant sides.
3. Use the inverse function, often accessed through a `SHIFT`, `2nd`, or `INV` key.
4. Check that the result is a plausible acute angle when working with an acute reference angle in a right triangle.
5. Round the final answer as instructed.

### Common mistakes
- Using ordinary sine instead of inverse sine to find an angle.
- Dividing the sides in the wrong order.
- Using the wrong side pair for the selected inverse function.
- Confusing inverse notation with a reciprocal.
- Forgetting degree mode.

### Week 6 practice
1. The opposite side is `6 cm` and the hypotenuse is `12 cm`. Find the acute angle.
2. The adjacent side is `5 cm` and the hypotenuse is `10 cm`. Find the acute angle.
3. The opposite side is `8 cm` and the adjacent side is `8 cm`. Find the acute angle.
4. The opposite side is `7 cm` and the hypotenuse is `11 cm`. Find the angle to one decimal place.
5. The adjacent side is `9 cm` and the hypotenuse is `13 cm`. Find the angle to one decimal place.
6. Explain the difference between `sin θ` and `sin⁻¹(x)`.

### Week 6 mastery check — answer key

1. `sin⁻¹(6/12) = 30°`.
2. `cos⁻¹(5/10) = 60°`.
3. `tan⁻¹(8/8) = 45°`.
4. `sin⁻¹(7/11) ≈ 39.5°`.
5. `cos⁻¹(9/13) ≈ 46.2°`.
6. `sin θ` calculates a ratio from a known angle; `sin⁻¹(x)` finds an angle from a known sine ratio.

# WEEK 7 — ELEVATION, DEPRESSION, HEIGHTS, AND DISTANCES

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Applying trigonometry to real-life problems.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Define angle of elevation and angle of depression.
2. Draw and label a diagram from a word problem.
3. Identify horizontal distance, vertical height, and line of sight.
4. Select an appropriate trigonometric ratio.
5. Solve basic height-and-distance problems and state reasonable assumptions.

## Lesson 7.1 — Angle of elevation

The **angle of elevation** is the angle between a horizontal line at the observer's position and the line of sight to an object above the observer.

For example, when a learner looks up from the ground to the top of a building, the angle between the horizontal and the upward line of sight is the angle of elevation.

## Lesson 7.2 — Angle of depression

The **angle of depression** is the angle between a horizontal line through the observer and the line of sight to an object below the observer.

For example, a person standing on a balcony looks down at a car. The angle between the horizontal and the downward line of sight is the angle of depression.

If the two horizontal lines are parallel, the angle of depression equals the corresponding angle of elevation at the other end of the line of sight. This follows from the geometry of parallel lines.

## Lesson 7.3 — A method for solving height-and-distance problems

1. Read the question carefully.
2. Draw a right-angled triangle representing the situation.
3. Mark the known angle and lengths.
4. Identify the unknown quantity.
5. Label opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse relative to the given angle.
6. Choose sine, cosine, or tangent.
7. Substitute and calculate.
8. State the answer with units and sensible rounding.
9. State any assumption, such as level ground or a vertical object, if relevant.

## Lesson 7.4 — Finding the height of a building

### Worked example

A learner stands `20 m` from the base of a vertical building. The angle of elevation from the learner's eye level to the top of the building is `35°`. Estimate the height of the building above the learner's eye level. Assume the ground is level.

The horizontal distance is adjacent and the height above eye level is opposite.

Use tangent:

`tan 35° = height / 20`

Rearrange:

`height = 20 × tan 35°`

`height ≈ 20 × 0.7002 = 14.0 m`

The height above the learner's eye level is approximately `14.0 m`.

If the question asks for the building's total height above the ground, the learner's eye height must also be included.

## Lesson 7.5 — Finding horizontal distance

### Worked example

A person observes the top of a vertical pole at an angle of elevation of `40°`. The pole is `8 m` high above the person's eye level. Find the horizontal distance to the pole, assuming level ground.

The height is opposite and the horizontal distance is adjacent.

`tan 40° = 8 / distance`

Rearrange:

`distance = 8 / tan 40°`

`distance ≈ 9.5 m`

## Lesson 7.6 — Line of sight as the hypotenuse

### Worked example

A kite is held by a straight string of length `25 m`. The string makes an angle of `30°` with the horizontal. Ignoring sag and the height of the person's hand, estimate the kite's vertical height above the hand.

The string is the hypotenuse and the vertical height is opposite the angle.

`sin 30° = height / 25`

`height = 25 × sin 30° = 12.5 m`

The vertical height above the hand is `12.5 m`.

### Assumptions and accuracy

Real-life questions often simplify the situation. Learners should note whether:
- The ground is level.
- The object is vertical.
- The observer's eye height is ignored or provided.
- The line of sight or string is assumed straight.
- Measurements are approximate.

### Week 7 practice
1. Explain the difference between elevation and depression.
2. A learner stands `15 m` from a tree. The angle of elevation to the top is `40°`. Find the height above eye level to one decimal place.
3. A pole is `10 m` high above an observer's eye level. The angle of elevation is `30°`. Find the horizontal distance.
4. A `20 m` cable makes an angle of `60°` with the horizontal. Find the vertical component of its length.
5. Why may eye height matter when estimating the total height of a building?
6. Draw a labelled diagram for a person looking down from a tower at a car.

### Week 7 mastery check — answer key

1. Elevation is measured upward from a horizontal; depression is measured downward from a horizontal.
2. `height = 15 × tan 40° ≈ 12.6 m`.
3. `distance = 10 / tan 30° ≈ 17.3 m`.
4. `vertical height = 20 × sin 60° ≈ 17.3 m`.
5. A calculation from eye level gives height above the eye, not total height from the ground.
6. Accept a clear diagram with horizontal, downward line of sight, angle of depression, tower, and car marked.

# WEEK 8 — INTEGRATION, CONSOLIDATION, REVISION, AND MASTERY

## Week overview

**Main topic:** Mixed problems, applications, revision, remedial/enrichment activities, and mastery assessment.

**Learning objectives**

By the end of the week, learners should be able to:
1. Select the appropriate method for a mixed trigonometry problem.
2. Decide when to use angle properties, Pythagoras' theorem, or a trigonometric ratio.
3. Solve multi-step problems.
4. Explain their reasoning and check whether answers are reasonable.
5. Identify areas needing further practice and demonstrate mastery in an assessment.

## Lesson 8.1 — Choosing a method

Before calculating, ask:

- Is the problem only about missing angles in a triangle? Use angle properties.
- Is it a right-angled triangle with two sides known and the third side required, without an angle needed? Consider Pythagoras' theorem.
- Is an acute angle and a side involved? Consider sine, cosine, or tangent.
- Are two sides known and an angle required? Consider inverse trigonometry.
- Is it a real-life height or distance problem? Draw a diagram first and then choose the relevant method.

## Lesson 8.2 — Mixed worked examples

### Example 1: Missing angle in a triangle

Two angles of a triangle are `38°` and `72°`. Find the third angle.

`third angle = 180° − 38° − 72° = 70°`

### Example 2: Pythagoras' theorem

A right-angled triangle has shorter sides `7 cm` and `24 cm`. Find the hypotenuse.

`c² = 7² + 24² = 49 + 576 = 625`

`c = √625 = 25 cm`

### Example 3: Finding a side with sine

A right-angled triangle has a `25°` angle and a hypotenuse of `18 cm`. Find the opposite side.

`opposite = 18 × sin 25° ≈ 7.6 cm`

### Example 4: Finding an angle with tangent

The opposite side is `9 cm` and the adjacent side is `12 cm`. Find the acute angle.

`θ = tan⁻¹(9/12)`

`θ ≈ 36.9°`

### Example 5: A multi-step problem

A ladder of length `10 m` rests against a vertical wall, making an angle of `65°` with the horizontal ground. Find:
(a) the height reached on the wall;
(b) the horizontal distance of the foot of the ladder from the wall.

For (a), the height is opposite the `65°` angle and the ladder is the hypotenuse:

`height = 10 × sin 65° ≈ 9.1 m`

For (b), the horizontal distance is adjacent and the ladder is the hypotenuse:

`distance = 10 × cos 65° ≈ 4.2 m`

Answers are approximate and should be rounded as instructed.

## Lesson 8.3 — Revision checklist

Learners should be able to:
- Classify angles and triangles.
- Use the `180°` triangle angle sum.
- Identify the hypotenuse, opposite, and adjacent sides.
- Apply Pythagoras' theorem only to right-angled triangles.
- Recall SOH-CAH-TOA.
- Calculate unknown sides using trigonometric ratios.
- Calculate unknown angles using inverse trigonometric functions.
- Solve basic elevation, depression, height, and distance problems.
- Use degree mode and round answers appropriately.
- Explain each step, not merely write a final answer.

## Lesson 8.4 — Remedial learning pathways

Use assessment results and the learner's actual error patterns to select revision. Do not assign the same generic remediation to every learner.

- **Weak on angles and triangles:** revisit Week 1, practise classifying angles and missing-angle questions, then check understanding again.
- **Weak on side labelling:** revisit Week 2 with labelled diagrams and several reference angles.
- **Weak on Pythagoras:** revisit Week 3, practise identifying the hypotenuse, then solve missing-hypotenuse and missing-shorter-side problems separately.
- **Weak on ratio selection:** revisit Week 4 and practise identifying the two relevant sides before calculating.
- **Weak on unknown sides:** revisit Week 5, separating sine, cosine, and tangent exercises.
- **Weak on unknown angles:** revisit Week 6, emphasizing inverse functions and calculator degree mode.
- **Weak on word problems:** revisit Week 7 and practise drawing diagrams before using formulas.

A learner should retry a mastery assessment according to the platform's configured attempt rules. Passing or unlocking must be determined by the backend.

## Lesson 8.5 — Enrichment activities

For learners who demonstrate mastery:
1. Solve height-and-distance problems requiring two steps.
2. Compare two methods where both are valid and explain which is more efficient.
3. Create a realistic word problem involving a ladder, ramp, building, tree, or kite.
4. Estimate an answer before using the calculator and compare the estimate with the result.
5. Explain a worked solution to another learner, using correct mathematical vocabulary.
6. Investigate how changing an angle while keeping the hypotenuse fixed changes the opposite and adjacent sides.

## Week 8 mixed mastery assessment

**Suggested format:** 15 questions, 1 mark each. Use the assessment system's configured pass mark and attempt limits. The answer key should not be exposed to the learner before submission.

1. Classify an angle of `135°`.
2. Find the third angle of a triangle with angles `45°` and `65°`.
3. A right-angled triangle has `angle B = 90°`. Which side is the hypotenuse in triangle `ABC`?
4. State Pythagoras' theorem.
5. Find the hypotenuse when the shorter sides are `9 cm` and `12 cm`.
6. Find the missing shorter side when the hypotenuse is `17 cm` and the other shorter side is `8 cm`.
7. Write the formula for `sin θ`.
8. Write the formula for `cos θ`.
9. Write the formula for `tan θ`.
10. Find the opposite side when the hypotenuse is `10 cm` and the angle is `30°`.
11. Find the adjacent side when the hypotenuse is `12 cm` and the angle is `60°`.
12. Find the angle when opposite `= 5 cm` and hypotenuse `= 10 cm`.
13. Find the angle when opposite `= 8 cm` and adjacent `= 8 cm`.
14. A person stands `10 m` from a vertical pole. The angle of elevation to the top is `45°`. Find the height above eye level.
15. Explain one assumption that may be made when solving a real-life height-and-distance problem.

## Week 8 mastery assessment — answer key for teacher/platform

1. Obtuse.
2. `180° − 45° − 65° = 70°`.
3. `AB` is opposite the right angle at `B`, so `AB` is the hypotenuse.
4. `a² + b² = c²`, where `c` is the hypotenuse.
5. `√(9² + 12²) = 15 cm`.
6. `√(17² − 8²) = √225 = 15 cm`.
7. `sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse`.
8. `cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse`.
9. `tan θ = opposite/adjacent`.
10. `10 × sin 30° = 5 cm`.
11. `12 × cos 60° = 6 cm`.
12. `sin⁻¹(5/10) = 30°`.
13. `tan⁻¹(8/8) = 45°`.
14. `10 × tan 45° = 10 m`.
15. Accept reasonable assumptions, e.g. level ground, vertical pole, or eye height ignored if not supplied.
